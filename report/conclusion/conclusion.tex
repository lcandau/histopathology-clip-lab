\chapter{Conclusions and Future Work}\label{chp:conclusion}

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:concl-conclusions}

We now return to the six research questions posed in §\ref{sec:intro-rq}, working through them in turn after summarising the headline finding.

\noindent\textbf{Headline.} The image encoder's pretraining is the dominant lever in this study. Holding everything else fixed, swapping the general-purpose ResNet50 (ImageNet supervised) for PLIP's pretrained ViT-B/32 (histopathology image--text contrastive) improves external evaluation by $+0.067$ F1 on close-shift colon (NCT-CRC), $+0.027$ on different-hospital colon (Chaoyang), and $+0.335$ on different-source lung (LungHist700). None of the other five interventions tested moves the needle comparably. The DINOv2 and PLIP zero-shot controls together decompose PLIP's lung advantage into three additive steps of comparable magnitude: ViT + self-supervised pretraining ($+0.152$), histopathology pretraining specifically ($+0.087$), and LC25000 supervision on top ($+0.096$).

\subsection*{RQ1 --- Baseline behavior and failure modes}

The frozen general-purpose CLIP pipeline (ResNet50 + BERT) saturates LC25000 at $\sim 0.99$ macro-F1, transfers partially to colon external data ($0.866$ on NCT-CRC, $0.786$ on Chaoyang), and categorically fails on lung external data ($0.305$ on LungHist700, near 3-class chance). The lung failure is a regime change rather than a smooth degradation: both ResNet50-based variants collapse into a single-class predictor, routing $\sim 90\%$ of inputs to ``lung squamous cell carcinoma'' regardless of true class. See Results §\ref{sec:results-baseline}.

\subsection*{RQ2 --- CLIP's foundational-model premise}

\textbf{CLIP's foundational-model premise is validated under shift, not in-distribution.} On LC25000 CLIP and a plain cross-entropy classifier on the same frozen ResNet50 backbone tie at saturation, and on close-shift NCT-CRC they tie marginally with plain CE narrowly ahead ($+0.014$ F1). CLIP's edge appears only under heavier shift: $+0.064$ F1 on Chaoyang and $+0.024$ F1 on LungHist700. This comes down to classifier geometry: CLIP's bounded-cosine, L2-normalised, temperature-scaled head is more robust than a high-confidence softmax, which under shift degenerates into a single-class predictor (visible on Chaoyang and LungHist700 in the per-class signatures). See Results §\ref{sec:results-foundational-vs-plain}.

\subsection*{RQ3 --- Stain normalisation and generalisation}

\textbf{Stain normalisation's direction is data-dependent.} Macenko (with the NCT-NORM reference) applied at training, and the same transform applied at inference only on external datasets that arrive in their native stain palette, helps on Chaoyang ($+0.030$ F1) and LungHist700 ($+0.020$ F1) and regresses on NCT-CRC ($-0.022$ F1, which is already Macenko-normalised at release). The story is intuitive in both directions: when an external input genuinely sits in a different stain palette than the training distribution, applying Macenko at inference moves it closer to the model's training-stain space and the frozen backbone produces better features. On NCT, where the input was already approximately in the target stain space, training-time Macenko added a non-linear input-statistic shift that the frozen backbone could not absorb, and there was no gain at evaluation to compensate.

Even where Macenko helps, the magnitude is modest. The Chaoyang gain ($+0.030$) is comparable to the image-encoder lever ($+0.027$ for PLIP), and on LungHist700 it is an order of magnitude smaller ($+0.020$ for Macenko vs $+0.335$ for PLIP). Stain normalisation is a useful but not dominant lever. See Results §\ref{sec:results-stain}.

\subsection*{RQ4 --- Biomedical text encoder pretraining}

\textbf{Biomedical text-encoder pretraining produces no measurable transfer benefit at this scale.} None of the six cells (BERT / BioBERT / PubMedBERT $\times$ bare-name / composed-prompt) beats the baseline on NCT external evaluation; all land in a narrow $0.81$--$0.86$ macro-F1 band. Extending the best biomedical cell (PubMedBERT + name\_only) to Chaoyang and LungHist700 tells the same story --- it ties baseline on Chaoyang ($+0.009$, within noise) and regresses on NCT ($-0.011$) and LungHist700 ($-0.026$). On LungHist700 the model still collapses to a single-class predictor ($\sim 92\%$ squamous), the same failure mode as the baseline, only sharper. Three reasons stack up here: the trained projection head absorbs encoder-specific differences in starting embedding space; short class-name prompts do not expose biomedical-vocabulary richness; and CLIP's shift robustness is a property of the classifier geometry, not of the text encoder's domain knowledge.

A small secondary observation is worth keeping for anyone designing similar experiments: vanilla BERT prefers the longer \emph{composed} prompt while biomedical encoders prefer the bare \emph{name\_only} format. None of this flips any cell to winning, but it is a useful note for short-prompt design. See Results §\ref{sec:results-text-encoder}.

\subsection*{RQ5 --- Image encoder pretraining (the dominant lever)}

\textbf{The image encoder's pretraining is the dominant lever in this study.} Replacing ResNet50 with PLIP's pretrained ViT-B/32 (histopathology image--text contrastive) gains $+0.067$ F1 on NCT, $+0.027$ on Chaoyang, and $+0.335$ on LungHist700. None of the head, loss, or text-side interventions tested gets close. The DINOv2 control plus the PLIP zero-shot reference decompose the lung gain into a clean three-step ladder ($0.305 \to 0.457 \to 0.544 \to 0.640$), each step adding one of (ViT + general-domain SSL, $+0.152$), (histopathology pretraining, $+0.087$), or (LC25000 supervision on top, $+0.096$); the three contributions are of comparable magnitude. The colon side decomposes differently: only histopathology pretraining moves the needle there, and general-domain SSL ties baseline within noise. The lever's contribution scales with shift severity.

Set against the broader CLIP-for-histopathology literature, our finding aligns with where the field has placed its effort. Recent foundation models emphasise larger and better-curated pretraining corpora (PLIP~\cite{huang2023plip}, BiomedCLIP~\cite{zhang2023biomedclip}, CONCH~\cite{lu2024conch}, UNI~\cite{chen2024uni}, MUSK~\cite{xiang2025musk}) and stronger self-supervised paradigms (CLIP-style contrastive, DINOv2-style self-distillation, iBOT-style masked image modelling) --- the image encoder's pretraining is precisely the lever they invest in. At the frozen-backbone scale evaluated here, this study finds the same thing on the downstream side: only the image-encoder swap moves the headline metric meaningfully, and it does so most decisively on the hardest dataset (LungHist700). See Results §\ref{sec:results-image-encoder}.

\subsection*{RQ6 --- Generalisation across 3 external datasets and 2 organs}

PLIP's ViT-B/32 wins every external evaluation in the study (NCT $0.933$, Chaoyang $0.813$, LungHist700 $0.640$), and its lung-side advantage over the baseline ($+0.335$ F1) is an order of magnitude larger than its colon-side advantages ($+0.067$ on NCT, $+0.027$ on Chaoyang). The cross-organ asymmetry is itself a regime change: ResNet50-based variants transfer partially to colon ($0.78$--$0.88$ F1) but categorically fail on lung ($\sim 0.30$, near 3-class chance), while PLIP shows no such asymmetry. \textbf{Per-organ external reporting is therefore essential}: aggregating across datasets would have hidden the regime change.

\textbf{What unlocks cross-organ transfer is feature breadth, not specifically histopathology knowledge.} The DINOv2 control demonstrates this directly: DINOv2's self-supervised pretraining on $\sim 142$M diverse natural images contains no histopathology at all, yet it lifts LungHist700 from $0.305$ to $0.457$ and produces a balanced 3-class predictor where the ImageNet-supervised ResNet50 collapsed. Narrow ImageNet-supervised features are too brittle for cross-organ transfer; either massive general-domain SSL or domain-specific (histopathology) pretraining is enough to unlock it. Histopathology pretraining additionally amplifies the gain --- both on colon (where general-domain SSL ties baseline) and on lung (where it stacks on top of the SSL contribution) --- but it is not strictly required to break the cross-organ collapse. See Results §\ref{sec:results-image-encoder} and §\ref{sec:results-discussion}.

\subsection*{Cross-cutting takeaways}

Two observations cut across multiple RQs and deserve calling out. First, \textbf{LC25000 accuracy says nothing useful about how a variant behaves under shift}. Every trained variant we tested saturates LC25000 at $\sim 0.99$ macro-F1, while external macro-F1 across the same variants ranges from $0.28$ to $0.93$. A reader looking only at the training-source numbers would have called nearly all of our variants identical --- when in fact they differ by up to $0.65$ F1 on external data. The practical consequence: in this kind of saturated frozen-backbone setup, you need external evaluation to distinguish variants at all.

Second, \textbf{CLIP's robustness under shift appears to be primarily geometric rather than knowledge-driven, in the setups tested here}. RQ2 points in this direction: the same frozen image features, classified by CLIP's cosine head, beat the same features classified by a softmax head under shift. RQ4 points the other way: swapping in a biomedical text encoder --- adding more medical domain knowledge to the text tower --- does not produce a measurable transfer benefit. Together they suggest the cosine + L2 + temperature geometry does most of the work in our regime; the components themselves can be domain-agnostic. We stop short of a stronger general claim because we did not test text-encoder swaps under heavier shift than NCT-CRC for every cell, and we used a single seed per condition.


\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:concl-limitations}

The data side has three caveats. LC25000 is a single-institution release with no patient-level grouping or clinical validation, and although the three external datasets used here (NCT-CRC, Chaoyang, LungHist700) span two organs and a meaningful range of shift severity, each is itself a single-institution release. Two LC25000-internal split artefacts affect in-distribution but not external numbers: $1\,250$ source images $\times$ $20$ augmentations are scattered randomly across the $80/10/10$ split (likely the dominant inflater of the in-distribution F1 ceiling), and the canonical pre-dedupe split additionally lets $1\,280$ byte-identical duplicates cross train/test (effect $\sim 1$--$2$ F1 points). External evaluations are unaffected by both.

A few methodological choices set the boundary of what this study can conclude. Every variant we evaluated keeps the image backbone frozen and only trains a projection head and temperature on top. We did not fine-tune end-to-end --- this is the regime where a histopathology foundation model is typically used, but if you unfroze the backbone, which lever dominates could easily shift. We also ran each condition with a single random seed, so we do not have a variance estimate per cell. That matters most for LungHist700 (only $359$ images split $85$/$146$/$128$ across the three classes); putting bootstrap confidence intervals around the headline lung gap is the first item on our future-work list. Finally, external evaluation uses restricted argmax over the LC25000-mappable classes (2-class colon for NCT and Chaoyang, 3-class lung for LungHist700) --- richer multi-class scenarios on those datasets were not tested here.

A handful of per-experiment caveats round out the picture. The image-encoder comparison bundles axes rather than isolating them individually: as discussed in §\ref{sec:meth-image}, each step in the ResNet50 $\rightarrow$ DINOv2 $\rightarrow$ PLIP chain changes two of the three axes at once (architecture/objective on the first step, objective/domain on the second), so the contribution of pretraining objective is never separated from the contribution of pretraining domain in any single delta. A fourth encoder (a general-domain CLIP-style model trained on natural image--text pairs) would close this gap and is listed in §\ref{sec:concl-future}. The same comparison also confounds patch size: PLIP uses ViT-B/32 and DINOv2 uses ViT-B/14, so at $224 \times 224$ input they see $49$ vs $256$ patch tokens. DINOv2's smaller patches do not rescue the colon-side gap, which suggests patch size is not the dominant factor; but to fully isolate ``pretraining domain'' as a variable, the two ViTs would need matching patch sizes. The stain-normalisation experiment uses a single NCT-NORM reference patch for every external evaluation; picking a different reference could move the numbers. LungHist700's source bias has not been characterised, so part of the ResNet50 lung collapse could be a stain artefact on top of the architectural limit, not just architecture. And on the comparison side, we ran no human-expert or clinical validation, and no head-to-head against other histopathology foundation models (UNI, CONCH, Virchow-2, BiomedCLIP, MUSK, PathChat). That last one would have told us whether PLIP specifically wins, or whether any histopathology-pretrained backbone would --- it is purely a compute-and-time call, and it is on the longer-term future-work list.


\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:concl-future}

\subsection*{Medium-term}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Push the InfoNCE training to a larger effective batch (via gradient accumulation or a larger GPU) and check whether the shared-prompt false-negative regime documented in §\ref{sec:meth-clip} is actually binding here, or whether the temperature behaviour we observed would survive at a larger $N$.
    \item Try prompt-tuning methods (CoOp~\cite{zhou2022coop}, CoCoOp) on top of the frozen encoders --- a learned prompt might close some of the colon-side gap without touching the backbone.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Longer-term}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Controlled head-to-head against other histopathology foundation models: UNI~\cite{chen2024uni}, CONCH~\cite{lu2024conch}, BiomedCLIP~\cite{zhang2023biomedclip}, MUSK~\cite{xiang2025musk}. The current study tells us that PLIP wins under our specific setup, but not whether any histopathology-pretrained backbone would win equally well, or whether PLIP is doing something the others are not.
    \item Multi-institution training and evaluation --- the natural fix for the single-institution limitation flagged above.
    \item \textbf{Alternative encodings for whole-slide images.} Patch-level approaches like the ones in this thesis are practical but throw away a lot of slide-level context. One direction worth exploring is treating a WSI as a sequence of tiles (a ``time series'') and training a multimodal model that ingests this sequence alongside accompanying text --- a fundamentally different way of encoding the gigapixel input.
    \item \textbf{Self-supervised pretraining on The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA).} TCGA provides a large corpus of histopathology data without dense semantic labels --- a natural fit for self-supervised pretraining at scale before downstream classification or segmentation. This would build a histopathology-domain backbone under controlled conditions, complementing the off-the-shelf PLIP / CONCH / UNI backbones tested here.
    \item Returning to dense prediction with pathology foundation-model backbones --- the segmentation question that motivated the early framing of this thesis.
    \item Applying CLIP-with-short-labels to other low-label scientific domains.
\end{itemize}


\section{Personal reflections}
\label{sec:concl-personal}

Looking back at the broader arc of this work, the things that stand out are not the F1 numbers themselves. The first is how much the difficulty of the histopathology domain shaped every choice the project made. Annotated data at the scale we initially needed was expensive twice over (in direct cost, and in the time medical experts could otherwise have spent with patients), and that wall pushed the project from its original goal (a segmentation tool to help accelerate the pathologist's workflow) toward a more modest but honest one: characterising how the CLIP paradigm transfers when domain-specific data is scarce. That reframing was less satisfying on paper but turned out to be the right one.

The second is how rewarding the hands-on work with the foundation-model regime has been. At the start of the master's, foundation models were still a relatively new term; today the pathology-specific variants (PLIP, CONCH, UNI, BiomedCLIP, MUSK) form a small ecosystem of their own, and being able to engage with that ecosystem, from U-Net and ResNet50 baselines through to PLIP and DINOv2, has given me a working familiarity I would like to deepen professionally. The CLIP paradigm in particular is one I want to keep exploring in scientific-imaging domains beyond histopathology, where the open-vocabulary capabilities not exercised here (retrieval, prompt-tuning at inference, zero-shot to unseen classes) could meaningfully lower the labelling cost that shaped every choice in this work.

The third, and the one I value the most, is the iterative supervision of my tutors. Every checkpoint surfaced another angle on the same question: segmentation became classification; classification numbers became classification plus UMAP visualisations (with image-thumbnail overlays adapted from one of my tutors' own work, which showed what F1 alone left invisible); in-distribution comparison became external evaluation; single-encoder swaps became three-axis decompositions. The habit of engaging with a problem from multiple sides is, more than any single technique tested in this thesis, what I will carry forward from the master's.
