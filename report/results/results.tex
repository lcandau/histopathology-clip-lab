\section{Experimental Overview}
\label{sec:results-overview}

\subsection{Research questions}
\label{sec:results-rqs}

The chapter is built around six research questions, each targeting a single design axis of a CLIP-style histopathology pipeline: the baseline reference, the CLIP-versus-non-CLIP choice, the input-side preprocessing, the text encoder, the image encoder, and cross-dataset generalisation. Five of them (RQ1--RQ5) are answered by a dedicated experiment section in this chapter; RQ6 is cross-cutting and is addressed by the external evaluations run alongside every other experiment. The experiment matrix in Table~\ref{tab:experiment-matrix} below maps each RQ to the controlled comparison that addresses it.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1} --- What baseline classification and generalisation behaviour does a frozen general-purpose CLIP pipeline achieve on LC25000, and what failure modes appear?
    \item \textbf{RQ2} --- Does the CLIP paradigm validate its \emph{foundational-model} premise --- learning one task and generalising to tasks it was not specifically trained for --- relative to a non-CLIP classifier on the same image encoder?
    \item \textbf{RQ3} --- Does stain normalisation, an input-side preprocessing that targets a known shortcut in histopathology, help when evaluating on datasets the model was never trained on?
    \item \textbf{RQ4} --- Does using a text encoder pretrained on biomedical text (BioBERT, PubMedBERT), instead of generic BERT, improve performance under short class-name prompts?
    \item \textbf{RQ5} --- Does the image encoder's \emph{pretraining choice} matter? We test two alternatives to the ImageNet-supervised ResNet50 baseline: (a) a Vision Transformer pretrained on histopathology image--text pairs (PLIP), and (b) a Vision Transformer pretrained self-supervised on general natural images (DINOv2), which serves as a control to separate the contribution of ``ViT architecture and self-supervision'' from ``histopathology pretraining specifically''.
    \item \textbf{RQ6} --- How well does each variant generalise to data the model has never seen? We evaluate every variant against three external datasets across two organs that span shift severity: close-shift colon, different-hospital colon, and different-source lung.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Training source and external datasets}
\label{sec:results-ood-framework}

Every variant in this chapter is trained on a single source dataset and evaluated on three external datasets it has never seen during training. The training source is LC25000~\cite{borkowski2019lc25000}, introduced in Methodology §\ref{sec:meth-dataset}: every trained variant is fit on it, and the in-distribution test split yields the macro-F1 we refer to as \emph{training-source results} throughout the chapter. The three external datasets --- the source of every \emph{generalisation} number in the chapter --- are listed in order of shift severity:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{NCT-CRC-HE-7K}~\cite{kather2019nct}, close-shift colon: $1\,974$ scored patches over the \texttt{NORM} and \texttt{TUM} classes, mapping to \emph{benign colon tissue} and \emph{colon adenocarcinoma} in LC25000.
    \item \textbf{Chaoyang}~\cite{zhu2021chaoyang}, different-hospital colon: $4\,060$ scored patches (\texttt{normal} and \texttt{adenocarcinoma}; the \texttt{serrated} and \texttt{adenoma} classes have no clean LC25000 analogue and are excluded from scoring), mapping to the same two LC25000 colon classes as NCT.
    \item \textbf{LungHist700}~\cite{tabatabaei2024lunghist}, different-source lung: $359$ images at $20\times$ magnification (the $40\times$ subset is excluded), mapping to LC25000's \emph{benign lung tissue}, \emph{lung adenocarcinoma}, and \emph{lung squamous cell carcinoma}.
\end{itemize}

Full dataset provenance, acquisition details, and per-dataset preprocessing live in Methodology (§\ref{sec:meth-external-datasets}); the bullets above keep only what is needed to interpret the external-evaluation tables in the rest of the chapter.

Three protocol choices apply to every external evaluation that follows. First, \textbf{restricted argmax}: each variant exposes all $5$ LC25000 class prompts at inference, but the argmax is restricted to the LC25000 classes that map to the external dataset's organ ($2$ colon classes for NCT and Chaoyang, $3$ lung classes for LungHist700). Predictions into a non-target LC25000 class count as wrong but are reported separately when informative for confusion analysis. Second, \textbf{pure inference}: every variant reuses its LC25000-trained checkpoint without further adaptation; no fine-tuning is performed on the external datasets. Third, \textbf{per-dataset reporting}: results are presented per dataset rather than averaged. The motivation for that last choice is methodological --- aggregating across datasets would average a colon-side partial transfer ($\sim 0.79$--$0.87$ F1) with a lung-side categorical failure ($0.305$ F1 in the baseline), producing a single mean-OOD number that hides the cross-organ regime change that ends up driving the chapter's headline result in §\ref{sec:results-image-encoder}.

\subsection{Metrics and visualizations}
\label{sec:results-metrics}

Each variant in this chapter is reported with the same set of metrics and visualisations. The choices and what each is meant to surface:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Macro-F1} is the headline metric. It averages the per-class F1 scores equally, which is the right behaviour for the per-class precision--recall trade-offs that drive the chapter's substantive comparisons. Formal definitions are in Methodology (§\ref{sec:meth-eval}).
    \item \textbf{Accuracy, balanced accuracy, weighted F1, and per-class precision/recall/F1} are reported alongside macro-F1 for completeness, but the chapter's interpretive weight is on macro-F1 and the per-class numbers.
    \item \textbf{Confusion matrices} (raw counts and row-normalised by true class) are reported per variant and per dataset --- the most direct view of how a model distributes its errors across the available classes.
    \item \textbf{Training-source UMAP} projects LC25000 image embeddings and class-prompt embeddings into the same 2-D fit (cosine metric), letting the reader see cluster separation and the alignment between image clusters and prompt anchors.
    \item \textbf{Cross-distribution UMAP grid} (per external dataset, per variant) overlays LC25000 dots and external-dataset triangles in a single 2-D fit, asking whether the external dataset co-locates with the matching LC25000 class cluster or sits in its own region of feature space.
\end{itemize}

\section{Baseline: frozen ResNet50 and frozen BERT}
\label{sec:results-baseline}

\subsection{Configuration}
\label{sec:results-baseline-config}

The baseline pipeline pairs a frozen ResNet50 image encoder, taken from the standard ImageNet-supervised checkpoint, with a frozen BERT-base-uncased text encoder; only a pair of small projection heads --- a single $256$-wide \texttt{Dense} layer with no bias, followed by \texttt{LayerNorm} --- and a scalar temperature $\tau$ are trained on top. This is deliberately the most general-purpose CLIP one can assemble. ResNet50 is the canonical CNN baseline of the past decade and BERT-base is the canonical text encoder; neither has been exposed to histology during pretraining, and neither has been tuned for the task. The reason to start here is mechanical: every subsequent variant in this chapter changes exactly one piece of this configuration, and the resulting delta is only interpretable if the reference is something recognisable rather than something already adapted to the data.

Everything else is held constant across the variants that follow. The training data is the LC25000 dataset, with a $80/10/10$ train--validation--test split at random seed $42$; a single fixed composed prompt, \texttt{``A histopathology image of \{class\_name\}.''}, is used at both training and inference; the loss is symmetric InfoNCE; the optimiser is AdamW with learning rate $1 \times 10^{-3}$ and weight decay $1 \times 10^{-4}$; and training runs for at most $30$ epochs, with EarlyStopping (patience $5$, restore best weights) terminating it earlier on every run. The remaining design choices --- batch size, image and sequence dimensions, initial temperature, gradient clipping, learning-rate schedule, numerical precision, weight initialisation --- are not referenced again in this chapter and are gathered in the baseline hyperparameter table in Methodology (Table~\ref{tab:baseline-hyperparameters}).

\subsection{Training behaviour and in-distribution results}
\label{sec:results-baseline-training}

On the LC25000 test split, the baseline reaches a macro-F1 of $0.996$. The model has only two trainable projection heads and a scalar temperature $\tau$ on top of frozen encoders --- around $720{,}000$ parameters in total --- so a saturated in-distribution score would invite a memorisation reading without a check on the training dynamics. Figure~\ref{fig:baseline-loss} provides that check: the training and validation InfoNCE losses (equation~\eqref{eq:infonce-sym}) descend together over the $30$-epoch budget and stay close at convergence. The learnable temperature $\tau$ (equation~\eqref{eq:scaled-cosine}), initialised at the CLIP-paper value of $0.07$, \emph{rises} during training, peaks at $\approx 0.090$ around epoch $10$, and settles near $0.087$. This is the opposite direction from the CLIP paper's $\tau \to 0.01$ behaviour on uniquely-paired image--text data, and is consistent with our shared-prompt setup: when multiple images per batch share the same class prompt (the false-negative caveat in §\ref{sec:meth-clip}), softening $\tau$ reduces the penalty those false negatives impose on otherwise-correct alignments.

    \label{fig:baseline-loss}
\end{figure}

The embedding space the model converges to is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:baseline-umap}. Each LC25000 class forms a distinct cluster of image embeddings and the five class-prompt anchors (encoded through frozen BERT and projected to the same $256$-d space) sit at the centre of their corresponding clusters --- the expected post-InfoNCE alignment between text and image, with the trained projection heads doing the work that the frozen text encoder alone could not. The lung-adenocarcinoma cluster sits closest to lung squamous cell carcinoma in the 2D projection, foreshadowing the off-diagonal confusion-matrix mass discussed below.

\subsection{Generalisation to external datasets}
\label{sec:results-baseline-ood}

The baseline's macro-F1 on each external dataset, restricted-argmax over the target-organ LC25000 classes, is reported in Table~\ref{tab:baseline-ood}. The result splits in two. On the two colon datasets the baseline transfers partially --- $0.866$ on NCT-CRC-HE-7K, the dataset stylistically closest to LC25000's colon distribution, and $0.786$ on Chaoyang, where a different hospital, scanner, and staining protocol drag the score down by $\sim 0.08$ F1 even though the organ is unchanged. On lung the picture is qualitatively different: \textbf{$\mathbf{0.305}$ macro-F1 on LungHist700's 3-class problem, where chance is $\mathbf{0.33}$}. The colon-versus-lung gap dwarfs every within-organ shift in the table --- the central observation the rest of the chapter sets out to explain.

    Chaoyang (colon)                 & \textbf{0.786} & 0.796 & 0.782 & 0.791 \\ \hline
    LungHist700 (lung, 20$\times$)   & \textbf{0.305} & 0.390 & 0.383 & 0.301 \\ \hline

The lung results are not a noisy degradation of the colon transfer; they are a different failure mode. Per-class recall on LungHist700 breaks down as $0.89$ for lung squamous cell carcinoma, $0.07$ for lung adenocarcinoma, and $0.19$ for benign lung tissue. Back-calculated from the precision figures, \textbf{the model assigns ``squamous'' to roughly $\mathbf{84}$\% of LungHist700 inputs regardless of true class} --- and given that squamous accounts for only $36$\% of the ground truth, the near-chance macro-F1 is the direct consequence of that bias. Figure~\ref{fig:baseline-lung-umap} shows the matching feature-space signature: all $359$ LungHist700 samples collapse into a single tight cluster with no visible internal class structure. The frozen ImageNet-supervised ResNet50, never exposed to histology during pretraining, extracts no usable per-class variance from LungHist700 tissue, and the trained projection head has nothing to align with the three lung class prompts.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering

    \label{fig:baseline-lung-umap}

The Chaoyang dataset sits between these two regimes (Figure~\ref{fig:baseline-chaoyang-umap}). The two colon classes are partially separated in the projected space, and the Chaoyang triangles cluster in a region adjacent to --- but not co-located with --- the matching LC25000 colon clusters: enough signal for partial transfer, with a measurable cost from the cross-hospital distribution gap. Together with NCT and LungHist700, this exposes two distinct generalisation regimes for the experiments that follow. There is a colon regime, where frozen ImageNet features carry good-enough structure for partial transfer, and a lung regime, where they carry no usable structure at all. The central question the rest of this chapter is built around: is the lung-side failure about the encoder's architecture, about its supervised pretraining on natural images, or about the absence of histopathology data in pretraining? The DINOv2 control (general-domain self-supervised pretraining, no histology) and the PLIP backbone (ViT pretrained on histopathology image--text pairs) jointly decompose that question, and the headline answer arrives in §\ref{sec:results-image-encoder}.

\section{Stain normalisation}
\label{sec:results-stain}

\subsection{Configuration}

The stain-normalisation experiment keeps the baseline pipeline architecture identical to §\ref{sec:results-baseline-config} and changes only the image-input distribution. Every LC25000 training image is Macenko-normalised~\cite{macenko2009stain} against a single fixed reference patch: the first \texttt{NORM} tile from NCT-CRC-HE-7K, abbreviated ``NCT-NORM'' below.

The choice of reference matters operationally. NCT-CRC is distributed already Macenko-normalised against an unpublished Kather-team reference~\cite{kather2019nct}; by Macenko-normalising LC25000 onto an NCT-CRC tile, we land the training distribution in approximately the colour space NCT-CRC arrives in at release, which lets NCT-CRC be fed raw at evaluation and avoids the double-Macenko regime that historically zeroed out the OOD F1 in an earlier version of this experiment. Chaoyang and LungHist700, by contrast, arrive in their native un-normalised palettes, so for those two datasets the same Macenko(NCT-NORM) transform is applied at inference to bring them into the model's training-stain space. An alternative reference drawn from Chaoyang or LungHist700 --- the latter being the most stain-distinct of the three external datasets --- would have re-introduced the double-Macenko problem on NCT-CRC and required a separate retraining run for each reference tested; the NCT-NORM reference is therefore picked for methodological consistency rather than per-dataset optimisation, and reference rotation as an ablation axis is documented as a limitation in Chapter~\ref{chp:conclusion}.

Reinhard and Vahadane were also explored as alternative normalisations in an exploratory pass but did not advance to the full external-evaluation comparison. Vahadane's NMF solver fails to converge on a fraction of LC25000 patches --- visible in Figure~\ref{fig:stain-before-after}(d), where the bottom-row \texttt{colon\_aca} cell re-renders near-blank. Reinhard is a Lab-space colour-statistics transfer rather than a stain-decomposition method, included in the figure for visual comparison only. The setup decisions for the three methods are described in §\ref{sec:meth-stain}.

\subsection{Generalisation to external datasets}

On LC25000 the Macenko-trained checkpoint saturates at $0.986$ macro-F1, alongside the baseline and every other trained variant in this study --- in-distribution does not discriminate this lever, so the substantive comparison is the external evaluation reported in Table~\ref{tab:stain-3way}.

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \small
    \begin{tabular}{|l|c|c|c|}
    \hline
    \textbf{External dataset} & \textbf{Baseline F1} & \textbf{Macenko F1} & \textbf{Delta} \\ \hline
    NCT-CRC-HE-7K (close-shift colon)         & $0.866$ & $0.844$ & $-0.022$ \\ \hline
    Chaoyang (different-hospital colon)       & $0.786$ & $0.816$ & $+0.030$ \\ \hline
    LungHist700 (different-source lung, 20$\times$)  & $0.305$ & $0.325$ & $+0.020$ \\ \hline
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Macenko(NCT-NORM) vs baseline on the three external evaluation datasets.}
    \label{tab:stain-3way}
\end{table}

\textbf{The lever's direction is data-dependent.} On the two datasets that arrive in their native stain palette --- Chaoyang ($+0.030$ F1) and LungHist700 ($+0.020$ F1), both receiving Macenko at inference --- normalisation moves the input closer to the model's training distribution and the frozen ImageNet ResNet50 produces better features. On NCT-CRC the picture inverts: NCT was already in approximately the target stain space at release, so training-time Macenko introduced a non-linear shift the frozen backbone could not absorb, and the eval-time pass-through cannot recover the small cost paid ($-0.022$ F1).

The magnitude is modest, even where the lever helps. The $+0.020$--$+0.030$ F1 gains on Chaoyang and LungHist700 are real but small; by comparison, swapping the image encoder to PLIP (§\ref{sec:results-image-encoder}) gains $+0.027$ on Chaoyang and \textbf{$\mathbf{+0.335}$ on LungHist700 --- an order of magnitude larger}. At the frozen-backbone scale evaluated here, stain normalisation is a useful but not dominant lever, and the protocol lesson worth carrying forward is the asymmetric application: Macenko is applied at inference only on inputs that arrive in their native stain palette, since doubling on a pre-normalised target is what historically broke the OOD F1 in this experiment.

\section{PLIP zero-shot reference}
\label{sec:results-plip-zeroshot}

\subsection{Configuration}

The PLIP zero-shot reference uses PLIP's Vision Transformer (ViT-B/32) and PLIP's matching text encoder~\cite{huang2023plip}, both frozen and used straight from the model release. No LC25000 supervision is applied: no projection head is trained on our data, no temperature is learned, no checkpoint is fine-tuned. Class prediction proceeds by argmax of cosine similarity between an image embedding and the five LC25000 class-prompt embeddings, both produced by PLIP itself. Evaluation runs on the same LC25000 test split and the same three external datasets as every other variant in this chapter.

This experiment is a \textbf{reference point, not an ablation}. It isolates the value of pathology pretraining alone --- what an off-the-shelf domain-pretrained model gives on these tasks before any LC25000 supervision is layered on top. The pair-up with the PLIP backbone in §\ref{sec:results-image-encoder}, which keeps PLIP's image representation but replaces its prompt geometry with a projection head trained on LC25000, becomes the chapter's clearest decomposition of which pieces of pathology pretraining contribute what.

\subsection{LC25000 results}

On LC25000 the PLIP zero-shot pipeline reaches a macro-F1 of $\mathbf{0.700}$. This is the only variant in the chapter where the in-distribution number is informative: every other variant has a projection head trained on LC25000 that drives macro-F1 to $\sim 0.99$ saturation, whereas PLIP zero-shot has no such supervision, so the gap between $0.700$ and saturation directly measures how far an off-the-shelf domain-pretrained model is from a model that has actually seen the training distribution. Within PLIP zero-shot, the per-class F1 spread is wide --- $0.87$ on benign lung and $0.80$ on benign colon, dropping to $0.48$ on lung adenocarcinoma and $0.56$ on colon adenocarcinoma --- so the off-the-shelf model handles healthy/normal tissue patterns more reliably than cancer-subtype distinctions, with no LC25000 supervision to refine the latter.

In the in-distribution UMAP (Figure~\ref{fig:plip-umap}) the visual comparison actually favours the baseline: the trained CLIP pipeline produces tighter, more compact class clusters than the off-the-shelf PLIP encoder, because the baseline's InfoNCE training has explicitly pulled image embeddings toward their matching prompts on LC25000. PLIP zero-shot's image clusters are looser, with lung adenocarcinoma and lung squamous cell carcinoma bleeding into each other more visibly than in the baseline. PLIP's pretrained features nonetheless carry enough class-discriminative signal to reach $0.700$ macro-F1 with the off-the-shelf classifier alone; the in-distribution UMAP underplays the strength of those features, because their real advantage shows up under shift, not on the same distribution the baseline was trained for.

The visible weakness of the zero-shot reference, in panel (a), is on the text side. In PLIP's off-the-shelf embedding space the five LC25000 class-prompt embeddings collapse to a near-singular point in the UMAP projection --- the single labelled $\bigstar$ near the centre, with the other four prompt markers overlapping it. Every \emph{trained} variant in this chapter uses its projection head to pull the prompts apart and align each with its image cluster; without that step, the off-the-shelf prompts are too similar to one another to discriminate the five LC25000 classes. The $0.700$ in-distribution number reflects this asymmetry: \textbf{the pretrained image representation carries genuine histopathology signal}, but the off-the-shelf prompt classifier built on it caps how far that signal can be exploited.

\section{Foundational model vs plain classifier}
\label{sec:results-foundational-vs-plain}

\subsection{Configuration}

The plain-classifier comparison strips the CLIP paradigm from the baseline pipeline while holding the image side untouched. The frozen ResNet50 image encoder from §\ref{sec:results-baseline-config} is reused verbatim --- same checkpoint, same preprocessing, same $80/10/10$ split on LC25000 --- and everything CLIP-specific is removed: no text encoder, no projection heads, no learnable temperature, no contrastive loss. In its place, a non-CLIP closed-set head is layered on top: ResNet50 features pass through global average pooling, a single \texttt{BatchNorm} layer, and a \texttt{Dense(5)} output trained with sparse categorical cross-entropy. The optimiser, learning rate, and training budget mirror the baseline.

The point of this configuration is to isolate the CLIP paradigm itself as the experimental variable. Image encoder, training data, and adaptation budget are all held constant; the only thing that changes is the classification head --- a contrastive image-text matching against five prompts on one side, a softmax over five direct logits on the other. Any external-evaluation gap between the two therefore measures what the CLIP paradigm contributes once the underlying image representation is the same. This is the RQ2 test: does the foundational-model framing earn its complexity, or could a plain closed-set classifier match it under the same conditions?


\subsection{Generalisation to external datasets}

The two heads tie on the training distribution. Macro-F1 on the LC25000 test split is $0.996$ for the baseline and $0.995$ for the plain classifier --- both saturated at the ceiling that any variant with a head trained on LC25000 reaches in this chapter. The story is in the external columns of Table~\ref{tab:clip-vs-plain}. Plain cross-entropy is narrowly ahead on NCT-CRC ($-0.014$ F1 for CLIP), behind by a meaningful margin on Chaoyang ($+0.064$ F1 for CLIP), and behind by a modest margin on LungHist700 ($+0.024$ F1 for CLIP). \textbf{The CLIP paradigm's edge is invisible on the training distribution, narrowly negative on the closest external dataset, and grows with shift severity from there.}

\begin{table}[ht]
    \centering
    \small
    \begin{tabular}{|l|c|c|c|c|}
    \hline
    \textbf{Variant} & \textbf{LC25000} & \textbf{NCT} & \textbf{Chaoyang} & \textbf{LungHist700} \\ \hline
    Baseline (CLIP InfoNCE)   & 0.996 & 0.866          & \textbf{0.786} & \textbf{0.305} \\ \hline
    Plain classifier (CE)     & 0.995 & \textbf{0.880} & 0.722          & 0.281 \\ \hline
    $\Delta$ (CLIP $-$ plain) & $-0.001$ & $-0.014$ & $+0.064$ & $+0.024$ \\ \hline
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{CLIP (baseline) vs plain classifier on the same frozen ResNet50 image encoder, macro-F1 across the training-source test split and three external datasets.}
    \label{tab:clip-vs-plain}
\end{table}

The Chaoyang gap is not a uniform regression --- it has a concrete collapse signature in the per-class metrics. Plain CE reaches benign-colon precision $0.96$ but recall only $0.47$: the classifier is rarely predicting benign at all, but is right when it does. Its adenocarcinoma precision is $0.70$ with recall $0.98$ --- almost every patch is being routed to the cancer class. This is a near-degenerate ``predict adenocarcinoma'' regime on a 2-class dataset. The baseline CLIP variant on the same images stays balanced (benign $0.86$/$0.65$, adeno $0.76$/$0.92$): imperfect, but not collapsed. Same image features, same supervision, same evaluation --- only the classifier head differs.

The collapse comes from the classifier head, not from the image features themselves. The plain head learns one score per class and predicts the class with the highest score; training on LC25000 tunes those scores to be sharp, so the winning class beats the others by a wide margin on LC25000 patches. Under shift the learned scores stay where training left them, but the image features now sit in a different region of the embedding space --- and there one class often scores highest for nearly every patch, regardless of true label. The sharp softmax then routes almost all the probability mass to that single class. That is the single-class collapse seen on Chaoyang. CLIP's head, by contrast, scores cosine similarity between an L2-normalised $256$-d image projection and a $256$-d prompt embedding, scaled by a learned temperature $\tau$. Three properties of this geometry stabilise behaviour under shift:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Cosine similarity is bounded in $[-1, 1]$, removing the unbounded-margin room a softmax has to over-extrapolate.
    \item The L2-normalised space concentrates representations near the unit sphere, so samples that drift in absolute feature space remain closer in \emph{angular} terms to the prompt anchors.
    \item The learned temperature $\tau$, optimised end-to-end on training data, tempers extreme confidence without collapsing the ranking signal.
\end{itemize}

The plain CE head has none of these buffers.

This is a property of the CLIP paradigm rather than of any specific encoder choice. CLIP's head geometry was designed for the open-vocabulary regime, where new class prompts can be introduced at inference time without retraining; the same geometric choices --- bounded cosine, normalised features, learned temperature --- also turn out to dampen confidence drift under distribution shift on closed-set tasks like ours. The result validates RQ2's foundational-model framing in a specific way: \textbf{CLIP does not earn its complexity at peak in-distribution accuracy --- on LC25000 a plain classifier is indistinguishable --- but in robustness under shift}, with margins that scale with how far the external dataset sits from the training source. The open-vocabulary capabilities (retrieval, prompt-tuning at inference, zero-shot to unseen classes) are not exercised by the closed-set evaluation in this chapter and remain a parallel argument for the paradigm.

\section{Text encoder pretraining}
\label{sec:results-text-encoder}

\subsection{Configuration}

The text-encoder comparison keeps the entire baseline pipeline from §\ref{sec:results-baseline-config} frozen and changes only one component: the text model. The image encoder, both projection heads, the InfoNCE loss, the LC25000 split, and the AdamW training schedule are all reused unchanged. Three text encoders are compared --- \texttt{bert-base-uncased} (the baseline's general-purpose checkpoint), BioBERT, and PubMedBERT --- all BERT-family, all producing 768-d text projections, all using wordpiece tokenisation. The architecture is therefore identical across the three; the only thing that differs is the text corpus each encoder was pretrained on.

On top of the encoder swap, two prompt formats are tested: \textbf{name\_only}, where the prompt is just the bare class name (e.g.\ \texttt{``colon adenocarcinoma''}), and \textbf{composed}, the baseline's template \texttt{``A histopathology image of \{class\}.''}. The cross match gives a $3 \times 2 = 6$-cell grid. The point of testing prompt length is that biomedical pretraining mostly affects how the model handles medical vocabulary --- and that vocabulary lives in the class name itself, not in the surrounding boilerplate. If biomedical encoders carry an advantage at all, it should be sharpest when the prompt strips down to the class name. This is the RQ4 test: does swapping in a more domain-aware text encoder buy anything for histopathology classification, and does the prompt format change the answer?

\subsection{NCT-CRC grid (6 cells)}

The first sweep crosses all six cells against the closest external dataset, NCT-CRC. The full picture is in Table~\ref{tab:text-encoder}: LC25000 in-distribution saturates at $\sim 0.99$ for every cell (the LC25000 axis does not discriminate these encoders), and the NCT macro-F1 scores all land in a narrow $0.81$--$0.86$ band. The baseline (BERT + composed, equivalent to the §\ref{sec:results-baseline-config} configuration) sits at $0.866$. The best \emph{biomedical} cell --- PubMedBERT + name\_only --- reaches $0.855$, still $0.011$ below baseline. \textbf{None of the six cells beats the baseline on NCT.} The UMAP grid in Figure~\ref{fig:biomed-umap} confirms what the numbers say: cluster geometry looks qualitatively similar across all six cells, so the projection head is producing comparable class anchors regardless of the starting text encoder.

Three converging reasons explain why biomedical text pretraining doesn't translate into a measurable advantage here. First, the projection head absorbs encoder-specific differences. BERT, BioBERT, and PubMedBERT all start from different 768-d embedding spaces, but all three get pulled through the same trainable projection head onto LC25000's five class anchors --- with only five classes to separate, that head learns to map any reasonable starting embedding onto useful anchors, washing out the encoder differences in the process. Second, short class-name prompts don't expose biomedical-vocabulary richness. Strings like \texttt{``colon adenocarcinoma''} tokenise into 2--3 wordpieces that any BERT-family model handles competently; biomedical pretraining adds value most where domain vocabulary is dense (clinical notes, paper abstracts, multi-sentence text), and the prompts here aren't dense at all. Third, \textbf{CLIP's robustness under shift is geometric, not knowledge-driven} --- the cosine + L2-norm + temperature classifier established in §\ref{sec:results-foundational-vs-plain} is independent of which text encoder produces the prompt embeddings. A different encoder just moves the starting positions of the anchors; the projection head re-maps them anyway, and the geometry that protects against shift stays the same.

\subsection{Generalisation to Chaoyang and LungHist700}

Even though no biomedical cell beats baseline on NCT, it could still be that biomedical pretraining pays off under heavier shift. To check this, the best biomedical cell on NCT --- PubMedBERT + name\_only --- was extended to both Chaoyang and LungHist700 and compared against the baseline (BERT + composed). Table~\ref{tab:text-encoder-3way} shows the result: PubMedBERT + name\_only ties baseline on Chaoyang within noise ($+0.009$) and regresses on LungHist700 ($-0.026$), in addition to its $-0.011$ on NCT. \textbf{No measurable transfer benefit on any of the three external datasets.}

On LungHist700, both variants collapse to a single class --- and the collapse class is the same. Baseline routes most of its predictions to ``lung squamous cell carcinoma'' (the $\sim 84\%$ behaviour documented in §\ref{sec:results-baseline-ood}); PubMedBERT + name\_only collapses to the same class, only harder ($\sim 92\%$ of predictions). The text-encoder choice does not change the direction of the collapse --- only how strongly the model commits to that single class.

One secondary observation in Table~\ref{tab:text-encoder} is worth flagging because it might be useful for anyone designing similar ablations. Vanilla BERT prefers the longer \emph{composed} prompt ($0.863$ vs $0.821$ on NCT), while both biomedical encoders prefer the bare \emph{name\_only} format (PubMedBERT: $0.855$ vs $0.808$; BioBERT: $0.840$ vs $0.818$). The likely explanation is that biomedical pretraining made strings like \texttt{``colon adenocarcinoma''} carry strong signal on their own, so the generic ``A histopathology image of \ldots'' boilerplate just dilutes that signal --- whereas vanilla BERT benefits from the surrounding context to lock in a coherent embedding. None of this flips any cell to winning against baseline, but it's a useful note for short-prompt design.

\section{Image encoder pretraining}
\label{sec:results-image-encoder}

\subsection{Configuration}

The image-encoder comparison keeps the architecture, loss, prompts, train/val/test split, and frozen BERT text encoder from §\ref{sec:results-baseline-config} all unchanged. The only axis that moves is the image encoder's pretraining. Three encoders are compared head-to-head, all frozen --- the only trainable parameters in each variant are the projection head on top and the learned temperature $\tau$.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{ResNet50 (ImageNet, supervised)} --- the baseline's CNN, trained with class labels on natural images.
    \item \textbf{ViT-B/14 (DINOv2, self-supervised)} --- a Vision Transformer pretrained on $\sim 142$M curated general natural images via self-distillation, no labels, no histopathology.
    \item \textbf{ViT-B/32 (PLIP / OpenPath, contrastive)} --- a Vision Transformer pretrained on $\sim 208$k histopathology image--text pairs scraped from Twitter via a CLIP-style contrastive objective.
\end{itemize}

DINOv2 is the experimental control for this comparison. Without it, swapping ResNet50 for PLIP changes three things at once: the architecture (CNN $\rightarrow$ ViT), the pretraining objective (supervised classification $\rightarrow$ image--text contrastive), and the pretraining domain (natural images $\rightarrow$ histopathology). Any improvement could be attributed to any of the three. DINOv2 fixes this by holding architecture and the broad ``general-domain SSL'' axis constant while leaving only the domain different from PLIP. Concretely, if DINOv2 also breaks the baseline's lung collapse, part of PLIP's advantage is architectural rather than domain-specific; if DINOv2 stays at baseline-level performance, the advantage is specifically about histopathology pretraining.

One known confound is worth flagging upfront. PLIP uses ViT-B/32 (patch size $32 \times 32$) while DINOv2 uses ViT-B/14 (patch size $14 \times 14$). At the same $224 \times 224$ input, that gives DINOv2 $256$ patch tokens to PLIP's $49$ --- markedly different feature granularity. We track this as a potential confound throughout the section: if a result depends specifically on PLIP's pretraining, we should see DINOv2 fail to match it, despite DINOv2's higher spatial resolution. The patch-size axis is documented as a limitation in Chapter~\ref{chp:conclusion}.

\subsection{Generalisation to external datasets}

On LC25000 the three variants are indistinguishable: $0.996$ for PLIP, $0.996$ for the ResNet50 baseline, $0.989$ for DINOv2 --- all three saturate at the dataset's ceiling. Notably, DINOv2 saturates too despite having never seen labelled histology during pretraining: the LC25000 axis cannot tell these encoders apart. The story is in the external columns of Table~\ref{tab:image-encoder}. \textbf{PLIP wins on all three external datasets} ($+0.067$ on NCT, $+0.027$ on Chaoyang, $+0.335$ on LungHist700). DINOv2 is more selective --- $-0.011$ / $-0.010$ on the two colon datasets (within noise of baseline) but $+0.152$ on LungHist700. The lung gap is by far the largest single delta anywhere in this chapter; the rest of this section explains where it comes from.

Why does the encoder swap help so much more on lung than on colon? The ResNet50 baseline was pretrained on ImageNet --- supervised classification on natural images, with no histopathology. Whatever colon signal it carries to external data comes from LC25000 supervision finding ImageNet features that happen to correlate with the colon labels in LC25000 itself --- a correlation that transfers partially to NCT and Chaoyang (both colon, stylistically close) but not to LungHist700, where the in-distribution lung accuracy is essentially memorisation on the LC25000 lung subset. DINOv2's self-supervised pretraining on $\sim 142$M diverse natural images produces image features sensitive to a much wider range of local-texture variation than ImageNet supervision does, and that broader feature space is enough for LC25000 supervision plus the projection head to assemble a usable lung classifier --- even though DINOv2 itself has never seen a histology image. On colon, where the baseline's features were already adequate, DINOv2's extra breadth has nothing left to add.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:results-discussion}

\subsection{Synthesis across experiments}

Looking back across the chapter, the experiments fall into two camps. Most of what was tested --- stain Macenko normalisation, biomedical text encoders, prompt-format variation, and a plain closed-set classifier head --- produces changes within $\sim 0.03$ F1 of the baseline on the closest external dataset (NCT-CRC) and at most $\sim 0.03$ F1 of improvement on the harder external datasets. Stain Macenko is the only one of these input-side or head-side interventions that produces a positive gain under shift ($+0.030$ on Chaoyang, $+0.020$ on LungHist700, but $-0.022$ on NCT because NCT arrives already Macenko-normalised). The best biomedical text-encoder cell (PubMedBERT + name\_only) ties baseline on Chaoyang and regresses on both NCT and LungHist700 --- no measurable transfer benefit on any of the three external datasets.

The image-encoder swap is the exception. Replacing the ResNet50 baseline's pretraining moves the headline metric by $+0.067$ on NCT, $+0.027$ on Chaoyang, and $+0.335$ on LungHist700 --- the last one taking the lung result from a degenerate single-class predictor at $0.305$ to a functional 3-class classifier at $0.640$. The DINOv2 and PLIP zero-shot controls decompose that lung gain into a clean three-step ladder ($0.305 \to 0.457 \to 0.544 \to 0.640$), each step adding one of (architecture + general-domain SSL), (histopathology pretraining), or (LC25000 supervision on top); the three contributions are of comparable magnitude ($+0.152$, $+0.087$, $+0.096$ F1). \textbf{On LungHist700 the image-encoder lever is decisive --- the only intervention in this chapter that moves a non-functional predictor into a functional one.} On NCT it is the largest single positive lever; on Chaoyang it effectively ties stain Macenko ($+0.027$ vs $+0.030$).

\subsection{Secondary observations}

\textbf{Cross-organ regime change.} ResNet50 variants partially work on colon external data (macro-F1 $0.78$--$0.88$) but categorically fail on lung (around $0.30$, near 3-class chance). What's happening here is that LC25000 supervision can rescue ResNet50 features that happen to correlate with colon class labels in LC25000, but it cannot manufacture useful lung-tissue features inside a frozen ImageNet-pretrained ResNet50; the in-distribution lung accuracy is essentially memorisation. \textbf{Per-organ external evaluation is therefore essential}: aggregating across datasets would hide the regime change.

\textbf{Classification quality is not the same as representation alignment.} The baseline CLIP variant achieves cross-dataset \emph{cluster} alignment in feature space but lower classification F1; PLIP achieves the highest F1 but leaves external samples in their own region of feature space, not co-located with the LC25000 clusters. The contrastive objective optimises same-class-pair alignment; the cosine-classifier rule only needs alignment to the class-prompt direction. These are different geometric properties --- the right tradeoff between them depends on whether the downstream task is classification or representation-driven (retrieval, clustering, transfer).

\textbf{Plain-classifier flip under shift.} The plain CE classifier ties CLIP in-distribution and marginally wins on close-shift NCT, but loses Chaoyang ($+0.064$ F1 for CLIP) and LungHist700 ($+0.024$ F1 for CLIP). As §\ref{sec:results-foundational-vs-plain} established, the reason is that a high-confidence softmax classifier degenerates into a single-class predictor as shift severity grows, while CLIP's bounded-cosine geometry does not. \textbf{This supports RQ2's foundational-model framing: CLIP earns its complexity in shift robustness, not in-distribution accuracy.}

\subsection{Headline summary}

Table~\ref{tab:summary-headline} gathers the per-dataset macro-F1 for every variant evaluated in this chapter. \textbf{The headline finding is that the PLIP image encoder wins every external evaluation, and on LungHist700 it does so by an order of magnitude more than any other lever tested} ($+0.335$ F1 over baseline vs at most $+0.030$ for any other intervention). Chapter~\ref{chp:conclusion} returns to the limitations and future work.
