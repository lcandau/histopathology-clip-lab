\chapter{State of the Art}\label{chp:stateofart}
\epigraph{AI is undergoing a paradigm shift with the rise of models [...] that are trained on broad data at scale and are adaptable to a wide range of downstream tasks.}{Bommasani et al., 2021}

The contemporary AI paradigm has emerged from roughly a decade of deep-learning progress. AlexNet's 2012 ImageNet result~\cite{krizhevsky2012alexnet} ignited the modern convolutional-network era; deeper architectures such as VGG~\cite{simonyan2015vgg} and ResNet~\cite{he2016resnet} pushed image-classification accuracy further, while training from scratch (or fine-tuning from ImageNet) remained the dominant adaptation pattern. The 2017 introduction of the Transformer architecture~\cite{vaswani2017attention} reshaped natural-language processing first, with BERT~\cite{devlin2019bert} establishing transfer learning from large pretrained language models as standard practice, and then crossed back into vision through the Vision Transformer~\cite{dosovitskiy2021vit}. By the start of the 2020s, the convention of one-model-per-task had begun to give way to a different regime: pretrain on broad data at scale, then adapt with little task-specific supervision.

That regime has a name: \emph{foundation models}, large-scale models pretrained on broad data and then adapted to a wide range of downstream tasks~\cite{bommasani2021foundation}. The defining characteristic is breadth: a single pretraining run on heterogeneous data yields a model whose representations transfer across applications without task-specific architectures.

The most influential vision-language foundation model is CLIP~\cite{radford2021clip} (Radford et al., 2021), which trained two encoders --- one for images and one for text --- on $\sim 400$M image--text pairs scraped from the web, using a symmetric contrastive objective that aligned matched pairs in a shared embedding space. The resulting model can be applied zero-shot to new classification tasks simply by writing a prompt for each class. Concurrent work on ALIGN~\cite{jia2021align} (Jia et al., 2021) demonstrated that the same approach scales to billion-pair corpora and noisier web text. Both efforts established the contrastive image--text paradigm as a default for general-purpose vision-language representations, and the body of follow-up work has since spread across captioning, retrieval, classification, and grounding.

In parallel, a separate line of work focuses on \emph{unimodal} self-supervision: learning visual representations without explicit labels or text. Early contrastive approaches such as SimCLR~\cite{chen2020simclr} and MoCo~\cite{he2020moco} demonstrated that augmentation-based contrastive pretraining on natural images could rival supervised pretraining on downstream tasks. A subsequent line of work --- starting with DINO~\cite{caron2021dino} and culminating in DINOv2~\cite{oquab2024dinov2} --- replaced explicit contrastive negatives with self-distillation between a student and a teacher network trained on different augmented crops of the same image. DINOv2 in particular has emerged as a strong default visual backbone: pretrained on $\sim 142$M curated natural images, it produces representations that transfer competitively to many downstream tasks without fine-tuning. This work uses DINOv2 as a control to disentangle ``architecture + general-domain self-supervision'' from ``domain-specific pretraining'' in the image-encoder comparison.

Computational pathology has tracked the broader vision shift described above, lagging it by roughly half a decade. Early pathology-specific deep-learning work, roughly 2014--2019, was dominated by per-tile supervised CNNs trained from scratch or from ImageNet on specific tissue-classification tasks~\cite{cruzroa2014idc,litjens2017survey,coudray2018lung}; the resulting models were tightly bound to the dataset they were trained on and rarely transferred across institutions. The field has gradually moved toward histopathology-specific foundation models that pretrain on large unlabelled corpora of pathology images (typically The Cancer Genome Atlas, supplemented with institution-specific archives or web-scraped material) and then expose features that downstream tasks consume frozen or with minimal fine-tuning.

Recent pathology foundation models split into two design philosophies. The vision--language line, of which PLIP~\cite{huang2023plip} (Huang et al., 2023) is the most directly relevant to this work, trains CLIP-style image and text encoders on $\sim 208$k pathology image--text pairs scraped from medical Twitter. CONCH~\cite{lu2024conch} (Lu et al., 2024) scales the same approach to a larger curated corpus and broader downstream coverage. The vision-only line dispenses with text and trains image encoders via self-supervision or masked image modelling on millions of slides: TransPath~\cite{wang2022transpath} (Wang et al., 2022) paired a transformer backbone with self-supervised pretraining on TCGA; Phikon~\cite{filiot2023phikon} (Filiot et al., 2023) used masked image modelling; UNI~\cite{chen2024uni} (Chen et al., 2024) scaled DINO-style self-distillation to a corpus of $\sim 100$k pathology slides. BiomedCLIP~\cite{zhang2023biomedclip} (Zhang et al., 2023) and the more recent MUSK~\cite{xiang2025musk} (Xiang et al., 2025) extend the vision--language approach to broader biomedical content beyond histopathology specifically.

Several open challenges sit alongside this body of work. There is no equivalent of ImageNet for pathology (no single benchmark agreed upon across the field), so cross-paper comparisons are difficult, and dataset biases (single-institution releases, scanner-specific signatures, augmentation-pair leakage) can drive reported performance more than any model improvement. Cross-institution evaluation is costly because most public datasets are themselves single-institution. And the compute required to pretrain at modern scale (millions of slides, hundreds of GPU-days) restricts foundation-model development to a small number of well-resourced groups. Most pathology-foundation-model papers focus on \emph{how} to pretrain a better model and report downstream classification numbers on a handful of standard benchmarks; the question of \emph{how much each ingredient of a CLIP-style histopathology pipeline contributes} is less directly addressed. This work is a small-scale empirical study addressing exactly that question: holding a single baseline fixed and changing one component at a time, so that downstream-classification deltas can be attributed to specific design choices, evaluated across two organs and three external datasets.
